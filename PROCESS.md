### How I Used AI on This Essay

## Abstract

**What I kept:** The core argument — that reading code is a distinct and essential skill for interaction designers, and that the cognitive habits formed through coding are inseparable from design practice.

**What I rejected:** The first draft was too long (~170 words) and ate into the word count allocated for the body. I cut it down to ~80 words, keeping only the essential claims.

**What I changed:** Removed the phrase "rather than write it" from the abstract's first sentence because the essay's argument is more nuanced than a simple reading-vs-writing dichotomy.

## Introduction

**What I kept:** The personal narrative about making interactive prototypes and getting stuck at the boundary between idea and implementation. The clay metaphor as the central analogy.

**What I rejected:** Any generic academic opening ("In today's digital age...") or hypothetical scenarios. The opening had to be personal and specific.

**What I changed:** The AI initially suggested framing the introduction around a broader industry trend (AI replacing designers). I changed it to focus on my personal experience because the assignment values specificity over generalization.

## Code as Material

**What I kept:** The specific project example — a browser-based pixel-art game with real-time API image generation and 3D isometric camera controls — is drawn entirely from my own experience. The AI helped me structure the narrative arc of the paragraph (description → problem → reflection → argument), but every technical detail is real. I also kept the clay analogy, since it is the paragraph's only bridge to Wiberg's material turn.

**What I rejected:** A hypothetical framing ("imagine you are building…") — the assignment wants specificity. A bloated clay analogy — trimmed to one comparison, keeping the asymmetry (code's resistance is inferential, it fails silently and totally). And the camera-orbit example, which now appears only in §4; duplicating it made both sections read as padding.

**What I changed:** Four things. Corrected Vallgårda et al. from 2017 to 2016 (NordiCHI). Cut the efficiency argument about the AI re-reading the codebase — it reads as a tool complaint and dates fast. Moved the thesis ("code is a design material, not a delivery mechanism") from the end to the front, so the examples do work instead of illustrating. Unified person and tense, and changed "last semester" to "autumn 2025."

## The Gap Between Description and Specification

**What I kept:** All three examples are drawn from my real experience: (1) particle effects requiring several rounds of revision, (2) camera controls going from twitchy to laggy through successive fixes, (3) the AI forgetting prior conversation context and reverting changes we had already agreed on. The progression from imprecise description → failed fix → lost specification mirrors the workflow I'm trying to name.

**What I changed:** Four things, all structural rather than stylistic. First, I added a one-sentence definition at the start — description names what is wanted; specification names the mechanism that produces it — because the title promised a gap the body never defined. Second, I gave the particle example a convergence point: it did not just fail repeatedly, it failed until I stopped describing appearance and named parameters, after which one change was enough. Without that moment the paragraph proves only that the AI was hard to use. Third, I reframed the forgetting problem: instead of reading as a complaint about context windows, it now reads as evidence that a specification living in conversation disappears when the conversation resets. Same fact, opposite rhetorical valence. Fourth, I added a criterion to the claim that these are design skills rather than programming skills — a skill counts as design here if it survives the failure of any particular language — so the sentence reads as a testable distinction instead of a relabelling.

**What I rejected:** Two things. The AI suggested adding a theoretical framework — Norman's gulf of execution or similar HCI concepts — which I rejected because the paragraph's force comes from concrete, sensory description: the reader should feel "slower → too slow → smoother → too laggy," not be told what it illustrates. I also rejected quantifying the particle revisions as "more than three rounds." That number was specific but not evidential — three rounds is not a threshold — so I either state the actual round at which convergence happened and cite the log, or drop the number entirely and keep only the shift from describing looks to naming parameters.

## *Conclusion

**What I kept:** The argument that the habits formed by working with code (debugging, constraint-thinking, iterative refinement) are design skills, not programming skills. The closing image of "holding a material" vs. "pointing at it from across the room."

**What I rejected:** Any attempt to introduce new examples or arguments in the conclusion. The conclusion should synthesize, not introduce.

**What I changed:** The AI initially ended with a forward-looking statement about AI becoming a "co-pilot" for designers. I changed it to a more personal note about what I hope to "leave with" from the course, which feels more authentic to the essay's voice.
