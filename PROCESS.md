### How I Used AI on This Essay

## Abstract

**What I kept:** The core argument — that reading code is a distinct and essential skill for interaction designers, and that the cognitive habits formed through coding are inseparable from design practice.

**What I rejected:** The first draft was too long (~170 words) and ate into the word count allocated for the body. I cut it down to ~80 words, keeping only the essential claims.

**What I changed:** Removed the phrase "rather than write it" from the abstract's first sentence because the essay's argument is more nuanced than a simple reading-vs-writing dichotomy.

## Introduction

**What I kept:** The personal narrative about making interactive prototypes and getting stuck at the boundary between idea and implementation. The clay metaphor as the central analogy.

**What I rejected:** Any generic academic opening ("In today's digital age...") or hypothetical scenarios. The opening had to be personal and specific.

**What I changed:** The AI initially suggested framing the introduction around a broader industry trend (AI replacing designers). I changed it to focus on my personal experience because the assignment values specificity over generalization.

## Code as Material

**What I kept:** The specific project example (H5 pixel-art game with API-connected image generation, speech synthesis, 3D camera controls) is drawn entirely from my own experience. The AI helped me structure the narrative arc of the paragraph — moving from description → problem → reflection → argument — but every technical detail is real.

**What I rejected:** The AI initially suggested framing this paragraph around a hypothetical project ("imagine you are building...") rather than using my real example. I rejected this because the assignment values personal specificity over generality. I also rejected its first draft of the "clay" analogy extension, which was too long and repetitive — it kept restating the same point about opacity in different words. I trimmed it to one tight comparison.

**What I changed:** The AI described the camera controls as "orbit controls" — technically accurate but jargon-heavy. I changed it to "drag the mouse to orbit, pan, and zoom" because the reader does not need to know the Three.js API name for the interaction pattern. I also added the efficiency argument (AI having to re-read the entire codebase for small fixes) based on my own workflow pain points.

## The Gap Between Description and Specification

**What I kept:** All three examples are drawn from my real experience: (1) particle effects requiring 3+ rounds of revision, (2) camera controls being "twitchy" then "laggy" through successive fixes, (3) the AI forgetting prior conversation context and reverting changes. The progression from "imprecise description" → "failed fix" → "total context loss" mirrors my own description of the workflow.

**What I changed:** I translated my experience of needing to repeat the same request more than three times before getting an acceptable result into "more than three rounds of back-and-forth to reach something I could accept" — keeping the specificity of the number while making it read naturally in English. I also rendered my feeling of losing all previous progress as "everything would unravel in a single response," preserving the emotional weight without being melodramatic.

**What I rejected:** The AI initially suggested adding a theoretical framework here — citing Norman's "gulf of execution" or similar HCI concepts. I rejected this because the paragraph's power comes from concrete, sensory description, not from abstract theory. The argument is stronger when the reader feels the frustration of "slower → too slow → smoother → too laggy" than when they are told "this illustrates the gulf of execution."

## *Conclusion

**What I kept:** The argument that the habits formed by working with code (debugging, constraint-thinking, iterative refinement) are design skills, not programming skills. The closing image of "holding a material" vs. "pointing at it from across the room."

**What I rejected:** Any attempt to introduce new examples or arguments in the conclusion. The conclusion should synthesize, not introduce.

**What I changed:** The AI initially ended with a forward-looking statement about AI becoming a "co-pilot" for designers. I changed it to a more personal note about what I hope to "leave with" from the course, which feels more authentic to the essay's voice.
